OVERVIEW

About this analysis

This report presents results for three datasets, forming a tree-cover gradient. All sites (112) contains every site with data for all predictor columns and at least four years of continuous observations. Tree cover ≥30 % (93) and Tree cover ≥50 % (65) are the same set after filtering on mean tree cover within a 500 m buffer. All three are built through an identical pipeline and share the same predictor structure, so they differ only in which sites they contain.

Compare datasets on RMSE, not R². Screening to higher tree cover removes between-site variance, so R² falls even where the model is more accurate — on the ≥50 % subset R² drops ~41 % versus the ≥30 % set while RMSE improves ~6.7 %. R² remains valid for ranking models within one dataset.

Cross-validation structures

Every result is available under two resampling structures, switchable at the top left:

Leave-one-site-out (LOSO)

Each site is held out in turn and predicted by a model trained on all remaining sites. Tests extrapolation to an unseen site. Primary structure.

Repeated LOSO-CV (3 × 80%)

Each held-out site is predicted three times, each from a different random 80 % subsample of the remaining sites; predictions are averaged. Tests whether a result depends on one particular split, and yields a per-prediction stability measure (pred SD in the metrics table).

The two agree on every conclusion — model-rank agreement ρ = 0.85–0.96, disturbance effect correlated at r = 0.966, same best learner in all six dataset × CV combinations. Repeated CV is slightly more optimistic (it averages three predictions) and has less statistical power, so LOSO is used as the primary structure and repeated CV as a robustness check. See Section 14.

Disturbance metrics

For visualisation and thresholding we define three disturbance metrics, all computed within a 500 m buffer around each tower from the deadwood / tree-cover maps:

Absolute mortality

Standing deadwood cover as a percentage of the total buffer area — the absolute amount of dead canopy.

absolute mortality = deadwood / area × 100 (%)

Relative mortality

Deadwood relative to tree cover — what % of the standing trees are currently dead (defined where tree cover ≥ 10 %, capped at 100 %).

relative mortality = deadwood / tree cover × 100 (%)

Relative disturbance

Combines standing deadwood and tree-cover loss relative to forest plus loss — captures both dead-standing and removed/lost canopy.

relative disturbance = (deadwood + loss×100) / (forest + loss×100) × 100 (%)

Random-forest models (M1–M8)

Models are evaluated under both cross-validation structures above, on identical site-years across all five responses (GPPsat, NEPmax, ETmax, uWUE, WUE). Models are built from four predictor blocks: C climate / meteorology, T plant traits, M memory (the response's own previous-year value), D disturbance metrics. The four pairs that differ only by D (M1↔M2, M3↔M4, M5↔M6, M7↔M8) isolate the effect of adding disturbance. Models M5–M8 come in raw and anomaly memory variants, and every model is run at a 12-month and a 24-month climate-lag window.

A note on exp02. That configuration forces each raw-memory model's own previous-year EFP into every tree split (always.split.variables). It lifts the raw-memory models substantially (mean R² 0.404 → 0.648) and leaves anomaly and no-memory models untouched. It is, however, a hand-placed structural privilege for one predictor block: RF Optuna reaches essentially the same raw-memory performance (0.644) with no forcing at all, purely by tuning mtry to 0.72–0.91 of predictors instead of ranger's sqrt(p) default. The forcing compensated for a hyperparameter pathology rather than expressing a scientific choice — which is why the reported results use XGBoost Optuna, the best-performing configuration that forces nothing. See Section 14.

Disturbance-level thresholds

To classify each site's disturbance level into Low / Mid / High for each metric, we compare three thresholding schemes (all computed on the per-site peak value):

Tertiles

Equal-sized groups split at the 33rd and 67th percentiles — each class holds ~⅓ of sites (balanced statistical power, ignores natural clustering).

Equal-width

The min-to-max range divided into three equal-width bins — reflects the distribution shape but yields unequal group sizes (most sites fall in the low bin).

Natural breaks

Cut points at mean ± 0.5·SD — Low below, High above, Mid in between; grounded in the variance structure and roughly balanced.

SECTION 3

Model performance & effect of disturbance on RMSE

Paired per-site RMSE (cyan = without disturbance, pink = with disturbance) for the four model comparisons (C vs C+D, C+T vs C+T+D, C+M vs C+M+D, C+T+M vs C+T+D+M) across memory type × window.

Reading the labels. Each pair carries three numbers: % the median paired change in per-site RMSE (negative = less error, green); stars a one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, H1: adding D reduces per-site RMSE, with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR applied across the 80 tests in the figure (*** q<0.001, ** q<0.01, * q<0.05, ns); and %↓ the share of sites whose RMSE improved. Sites are paired on SITE_ID, so both models saw identical training data for each held-out site. Full statistics (p, q, n, medians) are written to RMSE_disturbance_significance.csv beside the figure.

Two caveats. The effect size is the median of the paired per-site changes, not the difference of the two medians — the latter ignores the pairing and disagreed in sign with the paired statistic in half of the comparisons checked. And because LOSO models share training sites, the per-site errors are not fully independent, which makes p-values mildly anti-conservative; strong results are safe, marginal ones (q ≈ 0.05) should not be over-read.

Boosting is greedy, so it locks onto the dominant prior-year EFP immediately - exactly the deficiency exp01/exp02 diagnosed in the RF. XGBoost recovers that gain for free, without the always.split trick (all-sites GPPsat M6 raw 12m: RF 0.554 → exp02-tuned RF 0.723 → XGBoost 0.712). But where no dominant predictor exists - the anomaly and no-memory models, whose signal is spread thinly over ~240-440 weak correlated columns - bagging wins: RF averages that noise, boosting chases it and overfits slightly.

Result 2 - the attribution caveat

Better prediction bought a weaker-looking disturbance signal. In the raw-memory models XGBoost reassigns 14-25 percentage points of |SHAP| from Disturbance to Memory (e.g. all-sites M6 raw 12m: Disturbance 48.8% → 23.8%, Memory 11.2% → 42.9%), because the site's own previous-year value already encodes its identity, baseline level, and any disturbance before year t−1 - leaving less residual variance for the disturbance columns. This is a model-choice artefact, not evidence that disturbance matters less. The anomaly and no-memory models move only 4-7 points under the same swap, so if a disturbance conclusion has to be robust to learner choice, those are the defensible lens - and any estimate from a raw-memory model should be reported together with the learner and its memory handling.

SECTION 12

LightGBM - and what replicates across learners

A third learner on the same data, same variables, same LOSO folds: LightGBM (leaf-wise, histogram-binned gradient boosting). Its hyperparameters are matched to the XGBoost run wherever a direct analogue exists, so the remaining difference is the growth strategy - leaf-wise (best-first) rather than XGBoost's depth-wise. Select LightGBM in the toggle at the top to browse every figure rebuilt from its outputs.

Why a third learner

Section 11 showed XGBoost buying a large gain on the raw-memory models while roughly halving the SHAP share attributed to disturbance. That raised an obvious question: is that a property of boosting, or a quirk of one library? LightGBM answers it - it is an independent implementation with a different tree-growth strategy, so anything both boosters do together is a property of boosting, not of XGBoost.

Disturbance carries real predictive information - but only where raw memory is absent. Adding D buys roughly +0.08 to +0.14 R² in the no-memory and anomaly-memory models, and essentially nothing (0.00–0.03) once the response's own raw previous-year value is already in the model. All three learners agree, so this is a property of the data, not of the algorithm: the raw lag already encodes the site's state including whatever disturbance happened before year t−1, leaving little for D to add. It is the predictive-skill counterpart of the SHAP result below.

LightGBM tuned — a fair-configuration check

The LightGBM run above uses num_leaves = 31, min_data_in_leaf = 5. A grouped-5-fold-CV sweep showed that this is under-regularised at n ≈ 414 site-years — the complexity cap, not the leaf size, is the binding knob. Re-running with CV-selected complexity per response (num_leaves 3–15, min_data_in_leaf 5–20) gives the LightGBM tuned toggle option. Outputs are entirely separate; the original LightGBM results are unchanged.

With proper regularisation LightGBM converges onto XGBoost (0.245 / 0.249 / 0.670 vs 0.248 / 0.249 / 0.664) — so the earlier LightGBM deficit on the weak-signal models was a configuration artefact, not a property of the algorithm. Critically, RF still leads on the no-memory and anomaly models (0.293), so the bagging-beats-boosting result on weak diffuse signals survives — now demonstrated with a fairly-configured booster. The gains (+0.01 to +0.03) are smaller than grouped CV predicted (+0.04), as expected: CV trains on ~80 % of sites and LOSO on ~99 %, so configs chosen under less data slightly over-regularise for LOSO. None of this changes the disturbance effect, which is invariant across all six variants.

XGBoost tuned — a negative result worth reporting

To remove the remaining fairness caveat (RF and LightGBM had CV-tuned variants, XGBoost did not), XGBoost got the same treatment: exhaustive grid search over max_depth × min_child_weight under grouped 5-fold CV. It did not reliably help.

On all-sites more models got worse than better (66 of 120 vs 54). Grouped CV had predicted +0.036 R² on the weak-signal models; LOSO delivered −0.011.

Why: the training-size gap between selection and evaluation. Grouped 5-fold CV trains on ~90 of 112 sites and therefore prefers shallower trees; LOSO then trains on 111 and the CV-preferred max_depth 2–4 underfits relative to the shipped 6. The gap is smaller on the 93-site dataset, which is exactly where tuning does help. The same mechanism shrank LightGBM's CV gain (+0.042 → +0.021); here it flips the sign.

What it settles: the untuned XGBoost in this comparison was not disadvantaged — a proper tuning attempt found no headroom. So "RF beats boosting on weak diffuse signals" is demonstrated rather than assumed. And the disturbance effect remains unchanged, now across all seven variants.

The replication result

LightGBM lands between RF and XGBoost on skill, and essentially on top of XGBoost on attribution. Both boosters independently (i) recover the large raw-memory gain that the default-mtry RF misses, (ii) lose a little to RF on the anomaly and no-memory models, and (iii) move a large, similar block of |SHAP| from Disturbance to Memory in the raw-memory models. Two independent implementations agreeing means the attribution shift is a property of boosting itself, not an artefact of one library - which strengthens the warning in Section 11: a disturbance estimate taken from a raw-memory model is conditional on the learner, whereas the anomaly and no-memory models stay comparable across all three.

Cross-validation structure (top-left toggle)

Two resampling structures are available, and they change how the models are validated — never what data or predictors they see.

Extra diagnostic—SD across repetitions = prediction stability, which LOSO cannot provide
Hyperparametersidentical — each variant uses exactly its own configuration under both structures

Why it is worth having. Each repetition trains on ~89 sites, almost exactly the ~90 that the grouped-5-fold-CV tuning used. That makes it a direct test of the CV→LOSO attenuation seen in the tuned variants — and it confirms it: grid-tuned XGBoost, which was worse than untuned under LOSO (0.239 vs 0.249 on the anomaly models), recovers to 0.263 under repeated CV. The tuning was never wrong; it was being evaluated at a training size it had not been selected for. The boosters gain +0.015 to +0.031 on the weak-signal models here, while RF — which most needs maximum training data at mtry = ⌊√p⌋ — loses a little (raw memory 0.484 → 0.454). exp02 is the only variant essentially unchanged (0.703 → 0.704): a structural fix is robust to the resampling structure in a way that hyperparameter tuning is not.

Note on the SHAP sections. Sections 7, 8 and the SHAP views of Section 9 do not change with this toggle. SHAP is computed from a full-data model trained on every site, not from the CV folds, so the resampling structure cannot affect it — those figures are identical under both and the toggle intentionally resolves them to the same files.

Grid vs Optuna — what actually mattered. Optuna used TPE as the sampler only: every trial was scored by the same R code and the same grouped-CV objective the grids used, so the only difference is the search strategy and the width of the space. For RF it changed nothing (0.699 vs 0.693) because a single parameter, mtry, dominates and the grid already found it. For the boosters it helped clearly (+0.023 and +0.015 R² on raw memory) — not because TPE is cleverer, but because a 7-dimensional space lets learning_rate, nrounds and regularisation move together, which a 2-dimensional complexity grid cannot express. An exhaustive grid over that space would be >10,000 fits. Notably, exp02 still beats every hyperparameter search: a targeted structural change (forcing the memory column into every split) outperforms tuning, and is the only variant that costs nothing on the no-memory and anomaly models.

2 · Does disturbance matter more for forests? No evidence

Tested at site level (one value per site, pooled over all 9 learners, so no pseudo-replication), on all_sites which spans the full gradient:

  • Tree cover vs disturbance benefit: Spearman ρ = 0.044, p = 0.65 — no relationship
  • Forest (83 sites) −3.09% vs non-forest (29 sites) −7.09%: p = 0.163 — not significant, and pointing opposite to the hypothesis

Caution: pooling all site × pair × EFP rows gives "non-forest benefits more, p = 1e-08". That is pseudo-replication — 60 non-independent rows per site — and the effect disappears once aggregated per site. Do not quote the pooled version.

Conclusion: the disturbance effect is EFP-specific, not ecosystem-specific. It depends on which function is predicted (carbon yes, water no), not on ecosystem type.

4 · Learner — why not the most accurate one

RF_exp02 has the highest mean R² (0.372 vs XGB_optuna 0.358) but was rejected. Its always.split.variables = raw memory hard-codes a structural privilege for one predictor block. RF_exp02 and RF_base are identical without raw memory (0.234 both); the forcing is worth +0.244 R² there and nothing elsewhere.

The defence that exists: memory is the control (an autoregressive nuisance term), disturbance is the treatment; forcing the control is conservative — RF_exp02 shows the weakest disturbance effect of the RF family. Forcing disturbance instead would be circular.

But the forcing is unnecessary. RF_optuna reaches essentially the same raw-memory performance (0.644 vs 0.648) with no forcing — purely by tuning mtry to 0.72–0.91 of predictors instead of ranger's sqrt(p) default (~0.05 when p ≈ 449). With ~21 of 449 predictors sampled per split, RF picked the memory variable by chance ~5% of the time. The forcing was a workaround for a hyperparameter pathology, not a scientific statement.

XGB_optuna selected: highest R² of any non-forcing learner, hyperparameters from a defined TPE search space, disturbance effect mid-pack (−2.15%, 78.3% improved) so not cherry-picked, and disturbance SHAP share 26.7% — slightly above RF_exp02's 25.9%.

Caveat: RF_base has the highest disturbance SHAP share (35.6%) partly because it is the worst model — its raw-memory models underperform (0.404), so proportionally more attribution falls to disturbance. Not evidence that disturbance matters more.

5 · Six things to state explicitly in the manuscript

  1. Justify tc≥30 a priori — "the forest-relevant subset" — not because it showed the strongest effect. The other two datasets are in the appendix showing the same direction.
  2. The 2 raw-memory significant results are fragile. GPPsat 12m and WUE 24m (* under LOSO) drop to 0/10 under repeated CV. The defensible line: once raw EFP memory is included, disturbance adds no consistent improvement.
  3. ETmax is a consistent null — ns in every configuration, slightly positive in places. An honest null across 3 datasets × 9 learners × 2 CV structures is a result; report it.
  4. uWUE/WUE are borderline — significant at 24m, not 12m. Describe as weak and window-dependent.
  5. Do not compare R² across datasets. tc≥50's R² is depressed ~41% by range restriction (GPPsat sd 8.72 → 6.97) while its RMSE improves ~6.7%. Use RMSE across datasets; R² only for ranking models within one.
  6. Robustness sentence available: "The disturbance effect is invariant across nine learner configurations (RF, XGBoost, LightGBM, each with tuned and Optuna variants) and two resampling structures; the adopted configuration yields a mid-range estimate."
