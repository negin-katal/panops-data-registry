\section*{Scope of this draft}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dataset:} tree cover $\geq$30\,\% within a 100 to 500\,m buffer  \textbf{93 sites, 395 site-years}.
        The unfiltered network (112 sites) and a stricter $\geq$50\,\% subset (65 sites) exist and are held
        for the supplement.
  \item \textbf{Models shown:} \textbf{climate + trait} vs \textbf{climate + traits + disturbance} only.
        The pair differs solely by the disturbance block, so it isolates its contribution.
  \item \textbf{Learners:} Random forest, XGBoost and LightGBM, \textbf{all tuned with Optuna} (TPE, 50 trials
        per response, grouped 5-fold CV on disjoint site groups).
  \item \textbf{Windows:} 12-month and 24-month climate aggregation from center of growing season backward.
  \item \textbf{Cross-validation:} leave-one-site-out \emph{and} repeated leave-one-site-out
        (3 $\times$ 80\,\% of sites), both shown.
  \item \textbf{Responses:} GPP$_{sat}$, NEP$_{max}$, ET$_{max}$, WUE.

  \item \textbf{disturbance metrics}: For visualisation and thresholding we define three disturbance metrics, all computed within a 500 m buffer around each tower from the deadwood / tree-cover maps:

\textbf{Absolute mortality}
Standing deadwood cover as a percentage of the total buffer area — the absolute amount of dead canopy.
absolute mortality = deadwood / area × 100 (\%)
\\
\textbf{Relative mortality}
Deadwood relative to tree cover, what \% of the standing trees are currently dead (defined where tree cover $\geq$ 10 \%, capped at 100 \%).

relative mortality = deadwood / tree cover × 100 (\%)
\\
\textbf{Relative disturbance}
Combines standing deadwood and tree-cover loss relative to forest plus loss — captures both dead-standing and removed/lost canopy.

relative disturbance = (deadwood + loss×100) / (forest + loss×100) × 100 (\%)
 
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The report's Section-9 four-panel board, regenerated \textbf{without uWUE}, for
        \textbf{XGBoost--Optuna}, M4, at \textbf{both} climate windows. Panels:
        \textbf{A} $\Delta$RMSE by IGBP class, \textbf{B} disturbance SHAP \% by IGBP class,
        \textbf{C} tree cover vs disturbance benefit, \textbf{D} tree cover vs disturbance SHAP.
  \item \textbf{This is the direct evidence on the ecosystem-type question}, and it is largely negative.
  \item \textbf{Panels C and D are flat.} The loess trends against tree cover are close to horizontal with

  \item The 12\,m and 24\,m boards are near-identical in pattern, differing mainly in level.

\section*{Figure 6: Which \emph{direction} does disturbance push? (signed SHAP)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Figures 3--4 use $|$SHAP$|$, which says how much the disturbance block matters but not which
        way it acts. Here we recompute SHAP keeping the \textbf{sign}: per site-year we sum the signed
        SHAP across the 100 disturbance predictors --- the block's net push on the prediction, in
        response units --- and average over the site's years. \textbf{Below zero = disturbance pulls the
        prediction down.} XGBoost--Optuna, M4, both windows.
  \item \textbf{This is a dose--response, and it is the clearest result in the draft.} At
        \textbf{High}-disturbance sites the block pushes the carbon fluxes \emph{down}; at
        \textbf{Low+Mid} sites it pushes them \emph{up}.
  \item GPP$_{sat}$: High median $\mathbf{-1.15}$\,$\mu$mol\,m$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$ (71\,\% of sites negative,
        $q<0.01$) vs Low+Mid $+1.00$. NEP$_{max}$: High $\mathbf{-1.81}$ (79\,\% negative, $q<0.01$) vs
        Low+Mid $+1.29$.
  \item WUE separates most sharply: High $-0.28$ with \textbf{86\,\% of sites negative} ($q<0.01$),
        Low+Mid $+0.17$ ($q<0.01$).
  \item \textbf{ET$_{max}$ stays at zero} --- medians $-0.002$ and $+0.001$, both ns. The same null as in
        Figures 2 and 4, now shown to be a null in \emph{direction} as well as magnitude.
  \item \textbf{This is what Figure 4 could not show.} There, High and Low+Mid looked similar because
        both draw heavily on the disturbance block; the magnitudes are comparable but the
        \emph{directions} are opposite, and taking absolute values cancelled them.
  \item Absolute mortality separates less well than the two relative metrics --- consistent with
        mortality mattering relative to how much canopy was there to lose.
  \item \decide{does this replace Figure 4, or accompany it? It arguably supersedes it.}
  \item \decide{add the other two learners, or keep XGBoost only?}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Storyline}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Introduction}
    \begin{itemize}
    \item Terrestrial ecosystems regulate global \textbf{carbon, water, and energy balances}; forest functioning is closely linked to vegetation structure and functional traits.
    
    \item Increasing \textbf{drought, heat, and biotic disturbances} are causing widespread tree mortality, with potentially large consequences for ecosystem functioning.
    
    \item Mortality alters \textbf{canopy structure and leaf area}, affecting carbon uptake, evapotranspiration, and energy partitioning, with cascading effects on soils, nutrients, and vegetation recovery.
    
    \item The magnitude and trajectory of these impacts likely depend on \textbf{vegetation type and functional trait composition}.
    
    \item However, quantitative understanding of mortality impacts across ecosystems remains limited due to \textbf{spatial and temporal constraints of field observations} and limited integration of mortality and trait data.
    
    \item Advances in \textbf{remote sensing, machine learning, citizen science, and trait databases} now enable large-scale assessments of mortality and ecosystem responses.
    
    \item This creates an opportunity to quantify how mortality affects ecosystem functioning and identify \textbf{vegetation types that are more sensitive or resilient}.
\end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Results}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Ecosystem functional properties across the site network
        \item Disturbance improves prediction of carbon fluxes, but not water fluxes
        \item The disturbance effect is highly heterogeneous among sites
        \item Robustness
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Discussions}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Canopy mortality informs carbon uptake, not water partitioning
        \item The effect is functional, not ecosystem-specific
        \item Site-level heterogeneity and its interpretation
        \item Limitations
        \item Implications
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Methods}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Study sites and data selection
        \item Ecosystem functional properties
        \item Predictor blocks
        \item Model configurations
        \item Model training and tunings
        \item Cross validation 
        \item Statistical analysis
        \item Variable importance 
    \end{itemize}
    
\end{itemize}
    


\section*{Notes on the underlying results}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Statistics: one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank on per-site RMSE, sites as the unit of replication,
        Benjamini--Hochberg FDR within each figure. Effect size is the \emph{median of the paired per-site
        differences}, not the difference of medians, the two disagree in sign for some comparisons.
  \item Violin y-axes are zoomed to the 95th percentile for readability; no data are removed and all statistics
        use every site.
  
  \item Full results (three datasets, nine learner configurations, both CV structures, all 24 model
        configurations) are in the interactive report:
        \url{https://negin-katal.github.io/fluxVSmortality/v10/index.html}
\end{itemize}
