\section*{Introduction}
    \begin{itemize}
    \item Terrestrial ecosystems regulate global \textbf{carbon, water, and energy balances}; forest functioning is closely linked to vegetation structure and functional traits.
    
    \item Increasing \textbf{drought, heat, and biotic disturbances} are causing widespread tree mortality, with potentially large consequences for ecosystem functioning.
    
    \item Mortality alters \textbf{canopy structure and leaf area}, affecting carbon uptake, evapotranspiration, and energy partitioning, with cascading effects on soils, nutrients, and vegetation recovery.
    
    \item The magnitude and trajectory of these impacts likely depend on \textbf{vegetation type and functional trait composition}.
    
    \item However, quantitative understanding of mortality impacts across ecosystems remains limited due to \textbf{spatial and temporal constraints of field observations} and limited integration of mortality and trait data.
    
    \item Advances in \textbf{remote sensing, machine learning, citizen science, and trait databases} now enable large-scale assessments of mortality and ecosystem responses.
    
    \item This creates an opportunity to quantify how mortality affects ecosystem functioning and identify \textbf{vegetation types that are more sensitive or resilient}.
\end{itemize}

Which \emph{direction} does disturbance push? (signed SHAP)
\begin{itemize}
  \item Earlier versions of this figure used $|$SHAP$|$, which says how much the disturbance block
        matters but not which way it acts. Here SHAP is recomputed keeping the \textbf{sign}: per
        site-year we sum the signed SHAP across the 100 disturbance predictors --- the block's net push
        on the prediction, in response units --- and average over the site's years.
        \textbf{Below zero means the model predicts a LOWER value of the EFP at that site} --- not
        that it predicts less accurately. Accuracy is Figure 2, where negative $\Delta$RMSE means
        \emph{less error}; here negative means \emph{lower predicted carbon uptake}. M4, natural-breaks thresholds,
        Low+Mid vs High, all three Optuna learners, both windows.
  \item \textbf{A clear dose--response.} At \textbf{High}-disturbance sites the block pushes predicted
        carbon uptake \emph{down}; at \textbf{Low+Mid} sites it pushes it \emph{up}.
  \item \textbf{And the model is right to do so.} Sites where disturbance pushes the prediction down
        have nearly twice the mortality (25.2\,\% vs 16.8\,\%) and markedly lower \emph{observed}
        GPP$_{sat}$ (19.2 vs 30.7). Across sites the signed contribution tracks mortality
        ($\rho = -0.56$, $p = 8\times10^{-9}$) and observed productivity ($\rho = +0.73$).
        The two figures are consistent: disturbance improves accuracy (Figure 2) \emph{because} it
        correctly lowers predictions at damaged sites.
  \item GPP$_{sat}$ at High: $-1.15$ (XGBoost), $-0.89$ (random forest), $-0.92$ (LightGBM)
        $\mu$mol\,m$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$; 71--79\,\% of High sites negative. Low+Mid is positive in every
        learner ($+0.69$ to $+1.00$).
  \item NEP$_{max}$ at High: $-1.81$, $-1.29$, $-2.12$; Low+Mid $+0.83$ to $+1.49$.
  \item WUE is the most consistent of all --- High $-0.28$, $-0.27$, $-0.28$ across the three learners,
        with up to 86\,\% of sites negative.
  \item \textbf{ET$_{max}$ sits at zero} in both categories and all three learners ($\pm0.002$, ns) ---
        the same null as Figure 2, now shown to be a null in \emph{direction} as well as magnitude.
  \item \textbf{The direction replicates across learners:} 12--14 of 24 High-category tests significant
        per learner, and the sign never disagrees.
  \item \textbf{Why this replaces the magnitude version.} Plotting the disturbance \emph{share} of
        $|$SHAP$|$ made High and Low+Mid look similar, because both draw heavily on the block. The
        magnitudes are comparable but the directions are opposite, and taking absolute values cancelled
        exactly the contrast of interest.
  \item Absolute mortality separates less well than the two relative metrics --- consistent with
        mortality mattering relative to how much canopy there was to lose.
  \item \decide{keep all three learners, or only the reported one?}
  \item \decide{one window in the main text and the other in the supplement?}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Does it depend on ecosystem type or tree cover?}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The report's Section-9 board, without uWUE, XGBoost--Optuna, M4, both windows.
        \textbf{Panels B and D now show the net signed disturbance SHAP} (as Figure 4), not the
        $|$SHAP$|$ share. \textbf{A} $\Delta$RMSE by IGBP, \textbf{B} direction by IGBP,
        \textbf{C} tree cover vs benefit, \textbf{D} tree cover vs direction.
  \item \textbf{This reverses what the magnitude version showed.} On $|$SHAP$|$ the disturbance share
        was unrelated to tree cover (Spearman $\rho = 0.04$, $p = 0.65$). On the \emph{signed} value the
        relationship is strong and highly significant:
        GPP$_{sat}$ $\rho = +0.66$ ($p = 5\times10^{-13}$), NEP$_{max}$ $\rho = +0.64$
        ($p = 4\times10^{-12}$), WUE $\rho = +0.67$ ($p = 3\times10^{-13}$),
        ET$_{max}$ $\rho = +0.31$ ($p = 0.003$).
  \item \textbf{Panel D, read plainly:} at low tree cover disturbance pulls predicted carbon uptake
        \emph{down}; at high tree cover it pushes it \emph{up}. The sign flips at roughly 50\,\% cover.
  \item \textbf{Panel B says the same by class} (median net signed SHAP, GPP$_{sat}$):
        open and wet classes negative --- OSH $-4.84$, WET $-4.16$, CSH $-2.91$, WSA $-1.46$;
        forest classes positive --- MF $+1.94$, DBF $+1.06$, EBF $+0.60$, ENF $+0.26$.
  \item \textbf{So the earlier conclusion needs qualifying.} The \emph{magnitude} of the disturbance
        effect does not depend on ecosystem type; its \emph{direction} clearly does. Only the signed
        analysis can see this, and it is invisible in Figures 3 and 4 of earlier drafts.
  \item \textbf{Caveats.} WET carries $n=10$ sites and the tree-cover correlation uses all 93, so those
        are solid; OSH, CSH ($n=2$) and WSA, EBF ($n=1$) are not individually interpretable. It is also
        worth asking how much of this is mechanical --- at low canopy cover there is less to lose, and
        the disturbance predictors may encode something different.
  \item Panels A and C are unchanged and remain weak: the $\Delta$RMSE benefit does not track tree cover.
  \item \decide{does this change the framing from "functional, not ecosystem-specific" to a
        direction-vs-magnitude distinction?}
  \item \decide{drop the $n\leq2$ IGBP classes from panels A and B, or keep them with a caveat?}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Methods}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dataset:} tree cover $\geq$30\,\% within a 100 to 500\,m buffer  \textbf{93 sites, 395 site-years}.
        The unfiltered network (112 sites) and a stricter $\geq$50\,\% subset (65 sites) exist and are held
        for the supplement.
  \item \textbf{Models shown:} \textbf{climate + trait} vs \textbf{climate + traits + disturbance} only.
        The pair differs solely by the disturbance block, so it isolates its contribution.
  \item \textbf{Learners:} Random forest, XGBoost and LightGBM, \textbf{all tuned with Optuna} (TPE, 50 trials
        per response, grouped 5-fold CV on disjoint site groups).
  \item \textbf{Windows:} 12-month and 24-month climate aggregation from center of growing season backward.
  \item \textbf{Cross-validation:} leave-one-site-out \emph{and} repeated leave-one-site-out
        (3 $\times$ 80\,\% of sites), both shown.
  \item \textbf{Responses:} GPP$_{sat}$, NEP$_{max}$, ET$_{max}$, WUE.

  \item \textbf{disturbance metrics}: For visualisation and thresholding we define three disturbance metrics, all computed within a 500 m buffer around each tower from the deadwood / tree-cover maps:

\textbf{Absolute mortality}
Standing deadwood cover as a percentage of the total buffer area — the absolute amount of dead canopy.
absolute mortality = deadwood / area × 100 (\%)
\\
\textbf{Relative mortality}
Deadwood relative to tree cover, what \% of the standing trees are currently dead (defined where tree cover $\geq$ 10 \%, capped at 100 \%).

relative mortality = deadwood / tree cover × 100 (\%)
\\
\textbf{Relative disturbance}
Combines standing deadwood and tree-cover loss relative to forest plus loss — captures both dead-standing and removed/lost canopy.

relative disturbance = (deadwood + loss×100) / (forest + loss×100) × 100 (\%)
 
\end{itemize}
