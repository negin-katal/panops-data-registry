\abstract{EGU Abstract:
Tree mortality is increasing globally due to climate extremes, disturbances, and biotic stressors, with profound consequences for ecosystem carbon cycling. However, the extent to which spatial patterns and temporal dynamics of tree mortality influence the functioning of ecosystems as a whole remains poorly quantified. 
In this study, we investigate how tree mortality impacts  key ecosystem functional properties, focusing on light-saturated gross primary productivity (GPPsat) and maximum net ecosystem production (NEPmax). Ecosystem functional properties were derived from long-term, half-hourly eddy covariance measurements across a range of forest ecosystems. Spatially explicit information on forest cover and tree mortality was obtained from satellite-based predictions of the deadtrees.earth initiative, which integrates drone-based observations with Earth observation data to produce multitemporal mortality and forest cover estimates at global  scale.
We assessed whether including tree mortality information in addition to environmental drives improves the explanation and prediction of global and site-level variability in the flux-derived ecosystem functional properties; GPPsat and NEPmax. Model performance and variable importance patterns were compared between scenarios with and without forest cover and mortality dynamics to quantify the added explanatory power.
This study aims to provide the first systematic assessment of how spatially explicit tree mortality information contributes to ecosystem functional properties derived from eddy covariance data, and to evaluate whether integrating tree mortality observations can improve our understanding of the controls of ecosystem productivity and carbon balance under ongoing climate change.
}


\keywords{ecosystem functioning, tree mortality, eddy covariance, deadtrees.earth}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}
Terrestrial ecosystems play a fundamental role in regulating global carbon, water, and energy balances through complex interactions between vegetation structure, physiological processes, and environmental drivers (\cite{bonan2008forests, fisher2018vegetation}). Forest ecosystems, in particular, represent critical components of the global climate system, storing approximately 45\% of terrestrial carbon and influencing regional precipitation patterns through evapotranspiration processes (\cite{pan2011large, ellison2017trees}). The functional properties of these ecosystems, including carbon sequestration rates, water use efficiency, and energy partitioning, are intrinsically linked to vegetation characteristics such as leaf area index, canopy structure, and plant functional traits (\cite{wright2004worldwide, diazGlobalSpectrumPlant2016}). However, increasing frequencies of drought, heat stress, and biotic disturbances under changing climatic conditions have led to widespread tree mortality events globally, fundamentally altering ecosystem structure and potentially disrupting these critical ecosystem services (\cite{allen2010global, anderegg2013consequences}).

Tree mortality represents a key ecological process that can dramatically reshape ecosystem functional properties through multiple pathways. Following mortality events, immediate changes occur in canopy structure and leaf area, directly affecting light interception, evapotranspiration rates, and carbon uptake capacity (\cite{hicke2012effects, edburg2012cascading}). These structural changes cascade through the ecosystem, influencing soil microclimate, decomposition rates, and nutrient cycling patterns (\cite{breshears2005regional, adams2009temperature}). Furthermore, the replacement of dead trees by understory vegetation or different plant functional types can lead to long-term shifts in ecosystem functioning, as different vegetation types exhibit distinct physiological characteristics and resource use strategies (\cite{hartmann2018research, hammond2022global}). The magnitude and direction of these changes likely depend on the dominant vegetation type affected, as coniferous and deciduous forests, grasslands, and shrublands exhibit fundamentally different functional trait profiles and responses to disturbance (\cite{franklin1987tree, flower2015responses}).

Despite the recognized importance of tree mortality for ecosystem functioning, significant knowledge gaps persist regarding the quantitative relationships between mortality events and subsequent changes in carbon, water, and energy balances across different vegetation types. Traditional ecological studies have been limited by the spatial and temporal scales of field observations, making it challenging to capture the full spectrum of mortality impacts across diverse ecosystems (\cite{mcdowell2008mechanisms, larson2012tree }). Moreover, the lack of comprehensive datasets linking tree mortality events to plant functional traits has hindered our ability to predict ecosystem responses and develop mechanistic understanding of post-mortality functional changes (\cite{breshears2005regional}). This data limitation has been particularly pronounced for understanding how different plant functional trait combinations influence the magnitude and trajectory of ecosystem recovery following mortality events.

Recent advances in remote sensing technology, machine learning algorithms, and crowd-sourced data collection have opened unprecedented opportunities to address these knowledge gaps at landscape to global scales. The emergence of citizen science platforms and collaborative monitoring networks has dramatically increased the availability of field observations, enabling researchers to document mortality events across diverse ecosystems with greater spatial and temporal resolution than previously possible. In this context, the deadtrees.earth platform represents a significant advancement in mortality monitoring capabilities, providing a standardized framework for collecting and sharing tree mortality data across multiple vegetation types and geographical regions. This platform integrates crowd-sourced field observations with remote sensing data, creating comprehensive datasets that enable large-scale analyses of mortality patterns and their ecosystem consequences.

Building upon these technological advances, recent modeling efforts have begun to incorporate plant functional trait data to better predict ecosystem responses to environmental changes. Notably, Lusk et al. (\cite{lusk2026crowdsourced}) demonstrated how integrating plant functional trait databases with mortality observations can improve predictions of post-disturbance ecosystem functioning, revealing that trait diversity and functional redundancy play critical roles in determining ecosystem resilience following tree mortality events. Their work highlighted the importance of considering plant functional trait composition when assessing vulnerability to mortality and predicting subsequent ecosystem changes, providing a mechanistic framework for understanding functional consequences across different vegetation types.

Given these recent advances in data availability and modeling approaches, there is now an unprecedented opportunity to comprehensively assess the impacts of tree mortality on ecosystem functional properties across diverse vegetation types. The central questions driving this research are: What are the quantitative impacts of tree mortality on ecosystem carbon, water, and energy balances? Do these impacts vary systematically among different vegetation types based on their functional trait composition? And can we identify vegetation types that exhibit greater sensitivity or resilience?
 

\section{Results}\label{sec2}

\subsection{Overview of expected patterns}

We expect ecosystem functional properties (EFPs) to vary substantially across sites and years, reflecting differences in climate, vegetation characteristics, and disturbance intensity. In particular, we expect light-saturated gross primary productivity (GPP$_{sat}$) and maximum net ecosystem production (NEP$_{max}$) to decline with increasing tree mortality and canopy disturbance, although the magnitude of this response will likely vary among sites and forest types.

We further expect that models including forest cover and mortality-related variables will outperform models based only on meteorological and plant trait predictors. This improvement is anticipated to be strongest at sites with marked mortality events or strong temporal variation in canopy condition.

\subsection{Spatial and temporal variation in ecosystem functional properties}

Across the global network of forest eddy covariance sites, annual values of GPP$_{sat}$, NEP$_{max}$, ET$_{max}$, uWUE, and WUE are expected to show pronounced spatial variability. Part of this variability will likely be associated with broad climatic gradients, while another part may reflect structural ecosystem differences captured by community-weighted mean plant traits and forest cover.

We also expect interannual variation within sites, particularly in years affected by disturbance, drought, or canopy decline. Sites experiencing stronger forest cover loss or deadwood accumulation are expected to exhibit reduced productivity and carbon uptake capacity relative to less disturbed years.

\subsection{Patterns in forest cover, mortality, and deadwood metrics}

Forest cover and mortality-related variables are expected to show strong heterogeneity among sites. Some sites will likely show relatively stable canopy conditions, whereas others may exhibit clear year-to-year decreases in forest cover or increases in deadwood fraction within the 500~m tower buffer.

Among the disturbance indicators, we expect the most informative variables to be those capturing both the extent and intensity of canopy loss and deadwood accumulation, such as \texttt{loss\_area\_frac\_500m}, \texttt{loss\_sum\_pp\_500m}, and \texttt{deadwood\_increase\_sum\_pp\_500m}. These variables are expected to provide complementary information about recent mortality dynamics around the flux towers.

\subsection{Expected model performance across predictor sets}

We expect Random Forest models that include only meteorological variables (M1) to explain part of the variation in EFPs, especially for productivity-related variables that are strongly linked to climatic conditions. Adding plant traits (M2) is expected to improve performance by accounting for structural and physiological differences among ecosystems.

The strongest model performance is expected for models that additionally include forest cover and disturbance variables (M3). If tree mortality provides meaningful explanatory information beyond climate and trait variation, then M3 should show higher R$^2$ and lower RMSE than M1 and M2. This improvement is expected to be particularly visible for GPP$_{sat}$ and NEP$_{max}$, which are closely tied to canopy condition and carbon uptake.

\subsection{Expected contribution of tree mortality variables}

We expect the inclusion of mortality-related predictors to increase model explanatory power, although the magnitude of improvement will likely differ across response variables. The strongest contribution is expected for GPP$_{sat}$ and NEP$_{max}$, because tree mortality directly alters canopy structure, photosynthetic capacity, and stand-level carbon balance.

Variable importance analyses are expected to show that meteorological predictors remain dominant overall, but that mortality-related variables become particularly influential at disturbed sites or in years following canopy loss. This would support the hypothesis that tree mortality contributes meaningful additional information for explaining ecosystem functioning beyond climate and plant traits alone.

\subsection{Expected site-level heterogeneity}

The effect of tree mortality is not expected to be uniform across all sites. Instead, we anticipate strong site-level heterogeneity depending on background climate, vegetation type, disturbance history, and resilience capacity. Some sites may show clear reductions in GPP$_{sat}$ or NEP$_{max}$ following canopy loss, whereas other sites may exhibit weaker or delayed responses.

This heterogeneity is expected to highlight that the role of mortality depends not only on the amount of canopy loss itself, but also on the broader environmental and ecological context in which mortality occurs.

\subsection{Interim interpretation}

Taken together, the expected results support the working hypothesis that tree mortality is an important but spatially variable control on ecosystem functioning. While meteorological conditions and plant functional traits are expected to explain a substantial share of variation in EFPs, forest cover and mortality dynamics are likely to provide additional explanatory power, especially for productivity- and carbon-balance-related properties.

These anticipated findings would suggest that integrating remotely sensed mortality information into flux-based analyses can improve our understanding of how forest disturbance shapes ecosystem functioning under ongoing environmental change.

\section{Methods}\label{sec11}

\subsection*{Study sites and data selection}

We assembled a multi-network eddy covariance dataset drawing from FLUXNET, ICOS, AmeriFlux, NEON and OzFlux, covering the period 2019--2025. Sites entered the analysis if they met three criteria: (i) at least four consecutive years of valid half-hourly flux observations within the study window, (ii) availability of co-located forest cover and disturbance data from the deadtrees.earth platform \cite{mosig2026deadtrees}, and (iii) complete data across all predictor columns after benchmark harmonisation (below). This yielded 112 sites and 414 site-years.

Because the disturbance product characterises canopy mortality, we defined a tree-cover gradient by screening on mean tree cover within a 500\,m buffer, computed per site as the multi-year mean of \texttt{tree\_cover\_mean\_pct\_500m}. Three nested datasets were analysed (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). The $\geq$30\,\% subset is used for all analyses reported in the main text; it retains the forest and woodland sites for which canopy mortality is a meaningful predictor while preserving sufficient sample size and disturbance variability. The unfiltered and $\geq$50\,\% datasets are reported in the Supplementary Information as sensitivity analyses.

The reported dataset comprises evergreen needleleaf forest (ENF, $n=45$ sites), deciduous broadleaf forest (DBF, $n=26$), wetland (WET, $n=10$), mixed forest (MF, $n=6$), closed and open shrubland (CSH, $n=2$; OSH, $n=2$), evergreen broadleaf forest (EBF, $n=1$) and woody savanna (WSA, $n=1$), with a median of four site-years per site.

\subsection*{Ecosystem functional properties}

Annual ecosystem functional properties (EFPs) were derived from half-hourly eddy covariance observations following \cite{musavi2015imprint} and \cite{migliavacca_three_2021}. For each site-year we computed light-saturated gross primary productivity (GPP$_\mathrm{sat}$; $\mu$mol\,m$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$), maximum net ecosystem production (NEP$_\mathrm{max}$; $\mu$mol\,m$^{-2}$\,s$^{-1}$), maximum evapotranspiration (ET$_\mathrm{max}$; mm\,d$^{-1}$), underlying water-use efficiency (uWUE $=\mathrm{GPP}\cdot\sqrt{\mathrm{VPD}}/\mathrm{ET}$; g\,C\,mm$^{-1}$) and water-use efficiency (WUE $=\mathrm{GPP}/\mathrm{ET}$; g\,C\,mm$^{-1}$).

EFP estimation was anchored to the centre of the growing season (CGS), defined as the date of maximum daily GPP after smoothing, so that predictors describe antecedent conditions relative to peak carbon uptake rather than to an arbitrary calendar boundary.

\subsection*{Predictor blocks}

Predictors were organised into four blocks whose sizes are fixed across all models and datasets (Table~\ref{tab:blocks}).

\textit{Climate} (C). Monthly aggregates of vapour pressure deficit, air temperature, precipitation and incoming shortwave radiation, computed from on-site observations for each calendar month preceding the CGS peak (mean and 5th/95th percentiles for VPD and $T_\mathrm{air}$; mean and cumulative sum for precipitation; mean for radiation). Two window lengths were evaluated: the 12 months immediately preceding the CGS (143 predictors) and the full 24-month window (286 predictors). Both are reported.

\textit{Traits} (T). Twenty-one site-level community-weighted mean plant functional traits from the planttraits.earth database \cite{lusk2026crowdsourced}, covering hydraulic traits ($P_{12}$, $P_{50}$, $P_{88}$, $g_\mathrm{s,max}$, maximum rooting depth), wood structure (specific stem density, conduit density and diameter) and leaf morphology and chemistry (specific leaf area, leaf area, thickness, width, dry mass, C, N by mass and area, P, C:N, $\delta^{15}$N). Traits are time-invariant and capture baseline differences in functioning independent of recent disturbance.

\textit{Disturbance} (D). One hundred predictors derived from annual Sentinel-2--based forest cover and deadwood maps (deadtrees.earth v2-2 \cite{mosig2026deadtrees}). Eleven metric families --- absolute mortality, relative mortality, relative disturbance, new mortality rate, new deadwood gain, relative tree loss, and thresholded variants of the latter four --- were computed within circular buffers of five radii (100, 200, 300, 400 and 500\,m) delineated in the local UTM projection, at the current year and at a one-year lag.

\textit{EFP memory} (M). The previous year's value of the response variable itself, in two encodings: the raw lagged value (raw memory) and its $z$-score anomaly relative to the site mean (anomaly memory). Both are single predictors and are evaluated as alternatives, never jointly.

The main text reports M3$\rightarrow$M4 (climate and traits, without memory) and M7$\rightarrow$M8 with raw memory. These form a factorial contrast in which traits are always present and disturbance and memory vary independently, allowing the disturbance effect to be evaluated both in the absence of ecosystem memory and conditional on it.

To ensure that all models within a response variable are compared on identical data, the 12- and 24-month benchmark tables were harmonised to their common set of complete site-years before training; three variables with $>$85\,\% missingness ($R_b$, $R_{b,\max}$, aCUE) were excluded, as they are not predictors of interest and their retention would have discarded most site-years.

\subsection*{Learning algorithm and hyperparameter selection}

Results in the main text use gradient-boosted regression trees (\texttt{xgboost} \cite{chen2016xgboost}) with hyperparameters selected by Tree-structured Parzen Estimator search (Optuna \cite{akiba2019optuna}; 50 trials per response variable) over grouped five-fold cross-validation, in which folds comprise disjoint groups of sites so that tuning cannot leak information across the site boundary used for evaluation. The search space covered learning rate ($10^{-2}$--0.3, log scale), number of boosting rounds (100--1000), maximum depth (1--8), minimum child weight (1--20), row subsampling (0.5--1.0), column subsampling (0.2--1.0) and L2 regularisation ($10^{-3}$--50, log scale). Selected configurations varied by response, with maximum depth between 2 and 8 and learning rates between 0.011 and 0.039.

To establish that our conclusions do not depend on this choice, the identical analysis --- same data, same predictor blocks, same folds --- was repeated for nine learner configurations: random forests (\texttt{ranger} \cite{wright2017ranger}), XGBoost and LightGBM \cite{ke2017lightgbm}, each with default settings, a hand-tuned configuration and an Optuna-tuned configuration. These are reported in the Supplementary Information.

We note that the highest overall accuracy was obtained by a random-forest configuration that forces the raw memory predictor into every tree split (\texttt{always.split.variables}), which raises mean $R^2$ of the memory-containing models from 0.404 to 0.648. This configuration was not adopted, because such forcing imposes a structural privilege on one predictor block that is not applied to the others. An Optuna-tuned random forest attains statistically indistinguishable performance on the same models ($R^2 = 0.644$) without any forcing, by selecting \texttt{mtry} between 0.72 and 0.91 of available predictors rather than the $\sqrt{p}$ default ($\approx$0.05 at $p \approx 449$); the forcing therefore compensated for a hyperparameter setting rather than expressing a modelling decision.

\subsection*{Cross-validation}

Models were validated by leave-one-site-out cross-validation (LOSO-CV): all observations from one site are withheld while the remaining sites form the training set, repeated for every site. This preserves spatial independence between training and test data and estimates transferability to unseen sites \cite{roberts2017cross}. Performance was quantified by root mean square error (RMSE) and the coefficient of determination ($R^2$).

As a robustness check, every model was additionally evaluated under repeated leave-one-site-out cross-validation, in which each held-out site is predicted three times from independent random 80\,\% subsamples of the remaining sites and the predictions averaged. The two structures agree on every conclusion drawn here: model rankings correlate at Spearman $\rho = 0.85$--0.96 within dataset and response, the estimated disturbance effect correlates at $r = 0.966$ across all 1{,}620 model comparisons, and the same learner configuration is best under both structures in all three datasets. Repeated CV is marginally more optimistic, as averaging three predictions reduces variance, and has lower statistical power; LOSO-CV is therefore reported as the primary structure.

Because $R^2$ depends on the variance of the response, it is not comparable across the three datasets: screening to higher tree cover reduces between-site variance, so $R^2$ falls even where absolute error improves. Cross-dataset comparisons are therefore made on RMSE, and $R^2$ is used only to rank models within a dataset.

\subsection*{Quantifying the effect of disturbance}

The contribution of the disturbance block was assessed by comparing model pairs that differ only in its inclusion. For each site $i$ and each pair, we computed the per-site RMSE with and without disturbance and expressed the difference in relative terms,
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathrm{RMSE}_i \;=\; 100 \times \frac{\mathrm{RMSE}_{i,\,\mathrm{with\,D}} - \mathrm{RMSE}_{i,\,\mathrm{without\,D}}}{\mathrm{RMSE}_{i,\,\mathrm{without\,D}}},
\end{equation}
so that negative values denote improvement. Relative rather than absolute differences are used because the five EFPs differ by orders of magnitude in units.

Significance was assessed by a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the site-level paired RMSE values, testing whether including disturbance reduces error. Sites are the unit of replication; tests were paired by site identifier. Because each response variable and model pair constitutes a separate test, $p$-values were adjusted within each figure by the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure controlling the false discovery rate at $q < 0.05$. We report the median paired relative change and the proportion of sites improving alongside each test, since a small median shift can reach significance with a large number of sites.

Effect sizes are reported as the median of the paired per-site differences rather than the difference of medians. The two are not equivalent and disagreed in sign for 10 of 20 comparisons in preliminary analyses; only the former is consistent with the paired test.

\subsection*{Variable importance}

To identify which predictors drive model behaviour, SHapley Additive exPlanations \cite{lundberg2017unified} were computed with the \texttt{treeshap} package \cite{kozmarski2021treeshap} for models M4 and M8 under both memory encodings and both climate windows, from models fitted to the complete dataset. Predictors were assigned to the four blocks (Climate, Traits, Disturbance, Memory) and block-level importance was summarised as the share of total mean absolute SHAP value. Because these models are fitted to all sites rather than to cross-validation folds, SHAP results are independent of the cross-validation structure.
